For stays longer than **three months**, EU/EEA citizens are required to register
their residency at the **anagrafe** (registry office) of the comune where they
live. This is the EU equivalent of "getting your papers in order" — and, unlike
non-EU citizens, you do **not** need a residence permit to do it.

## What you must demonstrate

EU free-movement rules let you register if you fall into one of these categories:

- you are **employed or self-employed** in Italy; or
- you have **sufficient economic resources** for yourself (and family) **plus
  health insurance cover**; or
- you are **enrolled in study** and have health cover and resources.

You prove the relevant condition with supporting documents (a work contract, proof
of income, enrolment, insurance, etc.).

## The process

You have two channels, and the online one is often overlooked.

**Online, through ANPR.** The national registry runs a transfer declaration
(*dichiarazione di trasferimento*) for EU citizens and their family members. You
choose between "nuova residenza" and "residenza in famiglia esistente", enter
your details and those of anyone moving with you, give the property details, and
attach the documents.

Two details are easy to miss. Documents already held by another European public
administration can be pulled in directly through **OOTS** (the Once Only
Technical System) rather than uploaded. And you can add a phone number and the
hours you are reachable, so the address check can be arranged. After sending the
declaration you can re-enter the service at any time to see its progress.

**At the comune.** Filing at the anagrafe desk remains available, and many
comuni also accept the declaration by PEC or through their own online form — see
your city's page for which channels it actually operates.

Either way, the declaration goes to the anagrafe staff of the comune you are
moving to, who arrange a **residency-address check** — typically a visit by the
local police (*vigili*) to confirm you live where you say you do. Once confirmed,
you are entered in the population register and can obtain an **attestazione di
iscrizione anagrafica**.

## What it costs, and what happens if you don't

The declaration and the registration itself are **free**.

There is also **no deadline and no penalty**. ANPR states both plainly: there is
no final date for iscrizione anagrafica, and EU citizens and their family members
incur no financial penalty or other consequence for not registering, or for not
carrying their registration certificate during a check.

That is worth knowing, because it reframes the whole step. Registering is not a
compliance obligation with a clock on it — it is the key that unlocks the health
service, tax residency, your *carta d'identità* and local services. You do it
because you need what it opens, not because someone will fine you.

## The certificate of residence — and when you actually need one

Once you are registered, the *certificato di residenza* becomes available. Most
people who go looking for it do not need it.

**A certificate is valid only between private parties.** Art. 40 DPR 445/2000
says so directly: in dealings with public administration bodies and operators of
public services, certificates "sono sempre sostituiti" by a self-declaration
under artt. 46–47. Residence is expressly on the list of facts you may self-declare
(art. 46 lett. b), alongside citizenship, *stato di famiglia* and your codice
fiscale. A certificate issued for a private party must even carry a printed
warning, on pain of nullity, that it may not be produced to a public body.

So the practical rule is: if a public office asks you for a *certificato di
residenza*, it is asking for something the law says it must not require.

**When a private counterparty does insist**, ANPR issues 15 certificate types
online — residence, *stato di famiglia*, citizenship, existence in life and
others — for yourself or a member of your *famiglia anagrafica*. You sign in with
SPID, CIE or CNS, preview the document, and download it as a PDF or have it sent
to your email or *domicilio digitale*. Each carries a QR code and a qualified
electronic seal from the Ministero dell'Interno.

**It is not automatically free.** The 2021 launch waived the stamp duty, and that
is still what most write-ups say, but the current rule is narrower — the
certificate is free only when the stated use falls within a statutory exemption.
Otherwise you pay **€16** of *imposta di bollo* through a linked payment portal
before the certificate is released.

The exemptions ANPR lists are:

- a request from a *onlus* or a sports club;
- court proceedings;
- proceedings protecting a vulnerable person (*interdizione*, *inabilitazione*,
  *amministrazione di sostegno*);
- adoption, fostering or guardianship of a minor;
- separation or divorce proceedings;
- a street name or house number changed by the authorities;
- a foreign pension.

**On validity the two sources disagree**, so use the shorter figure when a
deadline is involved. Art. 41 DPR 445/2000 gives certificates of facts that can
change a validity of six months from issue; ANPR's own FAQ says certificates
issued through the portal are valid for three months. Historical certificates and
death certificates are not offered online at all — those still mean a trip to the
comune.

## Why it matters

Registered residency is what unlocks enrolment in the national health service
(Step 3), and is the reference point for tax residency, your *carta d'identità*,
and many local services.
